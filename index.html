TL;DR

AURA distills 50-step bidirectional speech-to-video teachers into 4-step causal students that stream talking-head video chunk by chunk with a rolling KV cache, with no audio look-ahead. It comes in two sizes: AURA 1.3B runs in real time on a single GPU (576p, 20.7 FPS for 16 Hz playback, 23× faster than its teacher), and AURA 5B streams 720p at 9.7 FPS, matching its TalkVerse teacher at 17× the speed.

Abstract

Overview

Current audio-driven video diffusion models can synthesize realistic talking-head videos but generally require dozens of diffusion denoising steps over multi-second video chunks, making them unsuitable for streaming applications such as voice assistants and virtual avatars, where low-latency and online causal generation are required properties. AURA generates video chunk by chunk with a rolling KV cache, requiring only the audio of the current chunk to start generating, rather than a multi-second audio look-ahead like bidirectional models. It comes in two sizes: AURA 1.3B streams 576p video in real time on a single GPU (20.7 fps for a 16 Hz playback rate), making it suited to interactive avatar applications, and AURA 5B streams 720p video at 9.7 fps. Our two-stage distillation pipeline first aligns the causal student with the teacher, by training it to match the teacher's trajectories with fewer diffusion steps and a chunk-wise sliding window causal attention mask, then applies Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD) with backward simulation to close the remaining quality gap. On standard talking-head benchmarks, AURA 1.3B runs 23× faster than its own bidirectional teacher and reaches a quality comparable to TalkVerse at a lower resolution, and AURA 5B matches its TalkVerse teacher at 720p while running 17× faster. To support reproducibility and further research on streaming avatar generation, we release the weights of both models along with a dataset of 30K synthetic videos and their precomputed teacher trajectories.

Method

How AURA works

Method figurestatic/images/method.png

Figure 2. Two-stage distillation pipeline. Stage 1 (ODE teacher forcing) regresses the causal student onto the frozen teacher's multi-step ODE trajectory. Stage 2 (backward-simulation DMD) unrolls the student without gradients, then matches teacher and critic scores on the student's own trajectory.

1

Chunked causal architecture

Three modifications turn a 50-step bidirectional teacher into a 4-step causal student: block-wise causal attention over chunks of 4 latent frames (Figure 3a), no motion-frame conditioning (the KV cache already carries temporal context), and per-chunk mixed diffusion timesteps (Figure 2, bottom middle). AURA 5B is distilled from TalkVerse (5B). AURA 1.3B is distilled from a 1.3B teacher that we train with the TalkVerse pipeline on Wan2.1 1.3B, with audio features from the streaming Mimi codec instead of Wav2Vec2.

2

Two-stage distillation

Stage 1 regresses the student onto its teacher's ODE trajectories (30K clips per model) via MSE. Stage 2 applies Distribution Matching Distillation with backward simulation — training on the student's own rollout states — to close the remaining few-step quality gap (Figure 2). Each stage trains its own LoRA adapters; the Stage 1 LoRA is merged before Stage 2.

3

Rolling KV cache inference

The student streams one chunk at a time with 4 denoising steps. Mixed-timestep training lets each past chunk be cached once (Figure 3b); a rolling window keeps only a fixed identity anchor (first chunk) and the most recent chunk, giving constant memory regardless of video length (Figure 3a). RoPE positions are re-indexed so that they stay within the range seen in training on unbounded streams.

Attention maskstatic/images/fig_mask.png

Figure 3(a). Sliding-window causal attention mask (training). Each chunk of 4 latent frames attends to itself, the preceding chunk, and the initial chunk as an attention sink.

KV cachestatic/images/fig_kvcache.png

Figure 3(b). KV caching with and without per-chunk mixed timesteps: naive caching scales memory with the number of denoising steps T; mixed-timestep training shares one cache across all steps (O(1) memory).

With 4 denoising steps instead of 50, AURA matches or improves on its TalkVerse teacher across most automatic metrics, and is comparable to the much larger Wan-S2V (14B, multi-step) on FVD, JEDi and audio–visual alignment. Both AURA models have the best identity preservation (CSIM) of all methods on both test sets. AURA 1.3B obtains the best FVD on HALLO3 Test (177.1) at an FID on par with AURA 5B, but is behind on lip-sync. Both are faster than all baselines: AURA 5B runs at 9.7 fps end-to-end, a 17× speedup over TalkVerse (30× on the diffusion transformer alone), and AURA 1.3B at 20.7 fps, faster than its 16 Hz playback — real time on a single H100, 23× faster than its own bidirectional teacher (17.3 fps at 768×512, still real time). While Hallo3 tops FID and JEDi on both test sets, qualitative inspection (supplementary material of the paper) shows it produces exaggerated mouth motion and recurrent hand artifacts that these distribution-based metrics fail to flag.

User study

To assess perceived quality directly, we run an anonymous human evaluation on the Mabyduck platform: 400 pairwise comparisons from 27 raters on the 100 HALLO3 Test clips, covering all pairs among AURA 5B, AURA 1.3B, Hallo3 and the TalkVerse teacher, along four axes — overall visual quality, lip-sync, reference fidelity, and naturalness — aggregated into per-axis Bayesian Elo scores (Figure 5). Both AURA models are preferred over Hallo3 on all four axes, confirming that Hallo3's distribution-metric lead does not translate into perceived quality. Against the non-causal, 50-step teacher, AURA 5B ranks higher on reference fidelity and is on par on visual quality, lip-sync and naturalness — despite being causal and 17× faster. AURA 1.3B also ranks above the teacher on reference fidelity, but below AURA 5B and the teacher on visual quality, which reflects its lower resolution, as well as on lip-sync and naturalness.

Videos become blurrier as the number of steps decreases, which distribution metrics barely capture: FVD and JEDi are best at 4 steps and degrade only slightly at 3 and 2 steps, FID is even marginally better at 3 and 2 steps, and all drop clearly at 1 step. Face sharpness makes the blur explicit: it decreases monotonically from 50.4 at 4 steps to 27.0 at 1 step. Identity and lip-sync remain essentially stable down to 2 steps; the lower warping error with fewer steps reflects the same blur rather than a real improvement.

Behind the demo

How the demo works

1

Unmute, with a face

Unmute is Kyutai's voice AI: a streaming speech-to-text model listens to you, a text LLM writes the answer, and a streaming text-to-speech model speaks it. Unmute's speech models are built on Mimi, the same streaming audio codec AURA 1.3B is conditioned on, so AURA follows the voice in the audio representation it was trained on and turns it into video chunk by chunk, with no audio look-ahead.

2

Smaller chunks for lower latency

A chunk can only be generated once all of its audio has been spoken, so the avatar lags the voice by at least one chunk plus the time to generate it. With the paper's chunks of 4 latent frames (1 s of video), this delay is about 2 s in our streaming setup. For the demo, we retrain AURA 1.3B (Stage 1 and Stage 2) with chunks of 2 latent frames (0.5 s), which brings it down to about 1.2 s while staying real time.

3

Same context as the paper model

Smaller chunks should not mean less context. Each 2-frame chunk attends to the 2 previous chunks and to 2 anchor chunks from the start of the stream, i.e. 4 latent frames of recent motion and 4 latent frames of identity anchor: exactly what the 4-frame model sees through its previous chunk and its anchor chunk (Figure 3a). Only the chunk being generated gets smaller.
